Bench tools

Four small instruments that run entirely in this tab. Each one says plainly what it can and cannot prove.

WebRTC leak test

WebRTC can reveal network addresses your VPN never sees. This opens a peer connection and lists every address candidate your browser offers up.

Local service probe

Probes a handful of common ports on this device itself (development servers, dashboards). A fast answer suggests something is listening; silence proves little — browsers blur the details on purpose.

Persistent-token test

Stores one random test token as a cookie, then checks whether it survives. If a token from an earlier visit is still here, sites can recognise this browser the same way.

How this all works

The short course behind the score — what an address reveals, where tunnels leak, and why your browser has a face.

Your IP address is a return address

Every connection you make carries a public IP address, because replies need somewhere to go. That single number is more talkative than it looks. It usually identifies your provider, the city your traffic enters the internet from, and whether you are on a home line, a mobile network or a data-centre machine. Sites log it by default; advertisers and data brokers trade in it; and because households share one address, it tends to identify a home rather than a person — which is still plenty.

Addresses come in two generations. IPv4 is the familiar four-number form; IPv6 is the longer hexadecimal form built for a world with more devices than IPv4 could number. Many connections carry both, and a privacy tool that hides only one of them has hidden neither — a mismatch this page checks for when it compares what different lookups report.

Your address also changes. Providers rotate residential addresses, phones pick up a new one as they move between towers and Wi-Fi, and a router reboot can hand you a fresh number. Treat it as a rough, shifting location tag — accurate enough to matter, never exact enough to be your doorstep.

Where VPNs and proxies actually leak

A VPN moves your return address: sites see the tunnel's exit instead of your provider. But three side channels routinely give the game away. The first is DNS. Before any page loads, your device asks a resolver to translate names into numbers; if those questions still go to your provider's resolver while your traffic rides the tunnel, an observer sees exactly what you look up, and the resolver's country may contradict your exit country outright.

The second is WebRTC, the browser plumbing behind voice and video calls. To set up fast peer connections it gathers candidate addresses — including, on some setups, the real local or public address the tunnel was meant to hide. The third is simpler: the tunnel drops. A brief reconnect, a network switch or a sleeping laptop can send traffic over the open connection for a few seconds, and a few seconds is enough to log an address.

Proxies leak differently. A proxy forwards requests rather than tunnelling everything, so anything outside the proxy's scope — other apps, other protocols, the DNS lookups — travels direct. And free proxies add a worse problem: the operator can read, log or alter unencrypted traffic passing through. A tunnel you cannot audit is a witness you chose yourself.

Fingerprinting: the face your browser can't take off

Block every cookie and a determined tracker can still recognise you, because your browser answers questions in a distinctive combination: the exact user-agent string, screen geometry, installed fonts, graphics hardware, how your device draws a test image on a canvas, audio processing quirks, time zone, language list, core count. Individually these are trivia. Combined, they narrow a visitor down to a handful of machines on earth.

That is why this page's score cares about consistency as much as concealment. A browser claiming a Berlin address, a Chicago clock and a single-language profile set to a language nobody speaks in either place is more memorable, not less. The strongest setups are boring: a common browser, a common language set, a clock that agrees with the address, and a resolver in the same country as the exit.

You can shrink the surface — mainstream browser, default window habits, resist exotic extensions — but you cannot remove it, and tools that randomise every answer on every visit often stand out more than a stable, ordinary fingerprint. Aim to look typical, not invisible.

Finding your address on your own devices

  • Windows: open Settings → Network & internet → your connection → Properties for the local address; the public one is what this page shows at the top.
  • macOS: System Settings → Wi-Fi → Details beside your network shows the local address.
  • Android: Settings → About phone → Status (or the Wi-Fi network's details) lists the device address on the current network.
  • iPhone / iPad: Settings → Wi-Fi → the ⓘ beside your network shows the local address; note the “private Wi-Fi address” toggle, which rotates a per-network hardware address.
  • Router: the router's admin page (often 192.168.0.1 or 192.168.1.1) shows the household's public address on its status or WAN screen — the same number every device behind it shares.

Remember the split: the address in those settings is usually the local one, meaningful only inside your network. The public one — the return address the internet sees — is the number in this report.

Questions

Straight answers, including the uncomfortable ones.

Is my score stored or sent anywhere?+
No. The lookups that identify your address are ordinary requests to public IP-lookup services — they see the same address any website would. The score itself is computed in this tab, from those answers plus your browser's own settings, and it leaves with the tab.
Why does my city look wrong?+
Location is inferred from the address, not measured from your device. Providers route traffic through regional hubs, mobile networks exit in another city entirely, and databases lag behind address reassignments. Treat it as “where your provider's network surfaces”, which is exactly what websites see too.
Does a VPN make this score 100?+
Not by itself. A good tunnel fixes the address and the provider line. The deductions that remain — resolver country, system clock, language set, WebRTC candidates, fingerprint surface — are precisely the leaks that undo real-world VPN setups, and they are all on your device, where the VPN can't reach.
Can a site see my local (192.168…) address?+
Ordinary web pages can't read it directly. WebRTC used to hand it out freely; modern browsers now mask it behind randomised mDNS names in most cases. The WebRTC test above shows you exactly what your browser currently offers.
What's the single most useful fix?+
Make your stories agree. Point DNS at a resolver in the same country as your connection or tunnel exit, keep your system time zone consistent with where your traffic appears to be, and don't run an exotic browser configuration. Consistency beats gadgetry.
Is hiding my IP address legal?+
Using a VPN or proxy is legal in most countries, though a few restrict or regulate the tools, and nothing about a hidden address makes other unlawful activity lawful. Check your local rules if you're unsure — this page is a measuring instrument, not legal advice.
